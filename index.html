Images are historical evidence.

They are evidence of what communities chose to render visible, of how they organized space and time, of what knowledge systems they transmitted visually, and what they allowed to disappear.

  • Close viewingSustained attention to what a single image does, and to what its viewers were trained to notice.
  • Testing scaleQuantitative evidence tests whether an observation is true for larger numbers of images.
  • Joined methodsResearch questions in which quantitative evidence is one part of the method, together with close viewing.

About

Current project: Drawing from the Crowd, led as Principal Investigator since 2024. In this citizen science project, volunteers locate Japanese woodblock prints (ukiyo-e) on a map to show which real places they depict. It builds on digital projects in art history I have been involved with since 2016. Two of them matter most. The first is the research platform of the British Museum’s Late Hokusai project (2016–2019), which connected data on Hokusai’s works in English and Japanese (built in the research software ResearchSpace, now maintained by Kartography). The second is Bilder der Schweiz Online (2020–2022), a project of the University of Zurich that linked art historical data on Swiss prints, drawings, and photographs and located them on a map through a citizen science workflow. I completed my PhD on Hokusai’s early drawing manuals at the University of Zurich in 2023, summa cum laude.

All of this work asks one question: how to make Japanese images, and the historical evidence they contain, accessible across languages and audiences, while retaining all their historical complexity. The projects combine art history with computer vision, reference data, and museum practice. They need partners and volunteers. Their goal is methods that other projects can adopt.

Path

I moved between museums and universities because each answers a different part of my question. Museums give direct work with objects and their audiences. Universities give the questions and the methods. Digital research connects the two: it makes collections usable for research and for the public at the same time.

During my BA in Zurich (2010–2015), I researched Japanese print and netsuke collections and co-curated exhibitions in Bern and Burgdorf. An internship at the Cabinet d’arts graphiques in Geneva (2013) and an exchange year at Doshisha University in Kyoto followed. My BA thesis asked how exhibitions can present Japanese prints to visitors from the different language regions of Switzerland.

The MA in Museum Studies at the University of East Anglia (2015–2016) took me to the Japanese Section of the British Museum. From 2016 to 2019, I developed the bilingual research platform of the Late Hokusai project there. That work showed me how much research on images depends on well-structured data. I began my PhD at the University of Zurich in 2018, during the project, and completed it in 2023. Alongside it, I coordinated Bilder der Schweiz Online (2020–2022) and created its citizen science workflow.

After the PhD, I worked in cultural heritage for the Canton of Graubünden (2023–2024) and became guest curator at the Linden-Museum Stuttgart (2023). Since 2024, my postdoc in Zurich has brought these strands together in Drawing from the Crowd.

Short bio for organizers

Stephanie Santschi is a digital art historian of early modern Japan and a Postdoctoral Fellow at the Chair of East Asian Art History, University of Zurich. She works on Japanese woodblock prints (ukiyo-e), Hokusai’s drawing manuals, and citizen science, in interdisciplinary and collaborative projects. She leads Drawing from the Crowd, a citizen science project in which volunteers locate Japanese landscape prints on a map. Her framework AI_d for AI-use policies in university teaching was published in Teaching in Higher Education in 2026. Before completing her PhD at the University of Zurich (2023, summa cum laude), she worked at the British Museum on the Late Hokusai project, where she developed its bilingual research database in the software ResearchSpace (now maintained by Kartography). She received the FAN Award of the University of Zurich in 2026.
